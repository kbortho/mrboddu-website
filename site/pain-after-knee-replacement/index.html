How painful is a knee replacement?

A knee replacement is a big operation, and some pain afterwards is expected. Modern pain relief keeps most people comfortable enough to walk and exercise from the first day. Here is what to expect.

In hospital

  • Most people have a spinal anaesthetic, which numbs the legs for several hours after the operation.
  • I usually inject local anaesthetic around the knee during the operation, in line with national guidance, to ease pain for the first day.
  • You will be given regular painkillers by mouth, so that you can walk and do your exercises.

At home

  • Pain is usually worst in the first 1 to 2 weeks, and improves steadily over the next 6 to 12 weeks.
  • Take your painkillers regularly at first, rather than waiting for pain to build. Taking them about 30 minutes before exercise helps.
  • If you are given opioid painkillers, such as codeine, tramadol or morphine, it is essential to take a laxative with them, because they commonly cause constipation.
  • Reduce your stronger painkillers as the pain eases. Most people need only simple painkillers after a few weeks.
  • A cold pack wrapped in a towel, for 15 to 20 minutes at a time, and resting with your leg raised can ease pain and swelling.
  • Aching at night is common for a few weeks. See sleeping after a joint replacement.

Longer term

Most people have much less pain than before their operation. Around 1 in 5 people still have some pain or are not fully satisfied a year after a knee replacement, so I will help you weigh this up before you decide. The knee keeps improving for up to a year.

Get medical advice straight away if pain is getting worse rather than better, or comes with a red, hot or leaking wound, a fever, or pain and swelling in your calf. In an emergency, call 999.
