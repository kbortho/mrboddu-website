Having both knees replaced at the same time

If arthritis is bad in both knees, it is natural to want both done in one go. I do not usually recommend this. For most people, replacing one knee and then the other a few months later is safer and makes recovery easier. This page explains why.

My usual advice

I generally advise replacing one knee at a time. The second knee is replaced at a separate operation, usually at least three months later, once you have recovered from the first.

Why recovery is harder with both knees at once

After a single knee replacement, you have a “good leg” to lean on while the new knee settles. When both knees are replaced together, you do not. Getting out of bed, standing up from a chair and walking with a frame or crutches are much harder in the first few weeks, and you are likely to need a lot more help.

A large Canadian study of more than 37,000 people who had both knees replaced found that, after a same-day operation, about 45 in 100 needed a stay in an inpatient rehabilitation unit rather than going straight home, compared with about 9 in 100 after separate operations. Blood transfusion was about twice as common (37 in 100 compared with 17 in 100).

The extra risks

Replacing both knees at once means a longer operation, a longer anaesthetic and more blood loss, and it puts more strain on the heart and lungs. The anaesthetic risks have to be taken seriously.

  • A review of 18 studies and almost 28,000 patients found that a same-day operation on both knees carried about twice the risk of death, a higher risk of heart complications and a higher risk of a blood clot on the lung (pulmonary embolism) than a single knee replacement.
  • In a large US study, 3 in 1,000 people died in hospital after having both knees replaced, compared with about 1 in 1,000 after one knee. Being over 75 was one of the strongest risk factors.
  • An international expert consensus concluded that a same-day operation on both knees carries more medical risk, and that safety must always come before convenience.

Partial knee replacements are different

A partial knee replacement is a smaller operation, with less blood loss and a quicker recovery, so getting moving is less of a problem even when both knees are done together. A review of 765 same-day partial knee replacements on both sides found them as safe as separate operations, and a National Joint Registry study of almost 2,000 matched patients found no higher risk of death than a partial knee replacement on one side. Even so, one UK study found more serious complications after same-day operations, so I still consider this only for carefully selected patients. See partial or total knee replacement.

When I might consider it

Patient selection is the key. I would only consider replacing both knees at the same time if all of these apply:

  • both knees are severely affected, ideally both suitable for a partial knee replacement
  • you are fit and well, without significant heart or lung disease, and not very overweight
  • the anaesthetist agrees, after assessing you, that it is safe
  • you understand the extra risks and have strong support at home for the first few weeks

If you are older, or have heart, lung or other medical problems, I will recommend separate operations.

Talking it through

At your consultation, I will examine both knees, review your X-rays and talk about your general health and what matters most to you. In most cases we will plan to replace the worse knee first, and then decide about the other knee once you have recovered.
