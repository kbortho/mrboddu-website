Knee pain: arthritis or a meniscal tear?

Knee arthritis and meniscal tears are the two commonest causes of knee pain in middle age, and they often occur together. Telling them apart matters, because the treatments are different.

Typical features

Meniscal tearKnee arthritis
How it startsOften after a twist, or gradually with ageGradually, over months or years
PainUsually on one side of the knee, often sharp with twisting or squattingAn aching pain, often worse with walking, stairs and getting up
Other symptomsCatching, or the knee locking so it will not fully straightenStiffness after rest, swelling, grinding, and the knee becoming less straight over time
Best first testExamination, and an MRI scan where it would change treatmentExamination and standing X-rays

Why scans can mislead

Meniscal tears become very common with age, and many cause no symptoms at all. A large study of MRI scans in people over 50 found that many of those with a tear had no knee pain. So a tear on a scan is not always the cause of the pain, especially when there is arthritis as well.

Why the difference matters

  • A tear after an injury, especially in a younger person or a knee that locks, may benefit from keyhole surgery, and some tears can be repaired.
  • A tear that has come on gradually usually does best with physiotherapy. Good-quality trials have found that keyhole surgery often adds little for these tears.
  • Arthritis is treated with exercise, weight management, painkillers and sometimes an injection, and with a knee replacement when these no longer help. Keyhole surgery does not treat arthritis, and national guidance advises against it.

Getting it right

At your consultation, I will ask how your symptoms started, examine your knee and look at the right scans, so that we can agree the treatment most likely to help. See meniscal tear and knee arthritis.
