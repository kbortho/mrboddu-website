Leg length and limp after a hip replacement

In the first few weeks after a hip replacement, many people feel that the operated leg is longer, or notice a limp. Both are common, and in most people they settle as the muscles recover. A true, lasting difference in leg length that causes problems is much less common.

The short answer

  • A leg that feels longer in the first few weeks is common, and it usually settles within a few months.
  • A limp is normal at first. It usually improves steadily with exercises over the first 3 to 6 months.
  • If a true difference remains, a shoe raise usually solves it. Further surgery is rarely needed.

Why your leg may feel longer

There is a difference between a leg that feels longer and a leg that is longer.

  • A leg that feels longer: before surgery, arthritis often makes the hip stiff and the muscles around it tight, and the pelvis tilts to compensate. After surgery, the hip moves freely, but it takes time for the muscles and the pelvis to adjust. Until they do, the operated leg often feels longer even when the leg lengths are equal. This usually settles over the first few months as you walk and do your exercises.
  • A leg that is longer: arthritis can wear away the joint and shorten the leg, so a hip replacement often restores some of the lost length. Sometimes a small amount of extra length is needed to make the new hip stable and reduce the risk of dislocation.

How I plan for equal leg lengths

Leg length is one of the things I plan most carefully. Before surgery, I measure your X-rays to plan the size and position of the implants. During the operation, I check the leg length and the stability of the new hip before I finish. My aim is to make the leg lengths as equal as possible while keeping the hip stable.

Why you may limp at first

The main cause of a limp after a hip replacement is weakness of the abductor muscles on the outer side of the hip, which hold the pelvis level when you walk. These muscles are often weak before surgery because of the arthritis, and they need time and exercise to recover after the operation.

  • Most people walk much better within the first few weeks, but it can take 3 to 6 months for the limp to go completely.
  • A review of studies on walking after hip replacement found that walking improves early after surgery, but some differences in walking speed and stride can still be measured at a year compared with people without hip problems. Most people do not notice these differences in daily life.
  • Regular exercises to strengthen the muscles on the outer side of the hip make the biggest difference. See exercises after a hip replacement.
  • Using your sticks or crutches for as long as you need them helps you to walk without a limp, rather than getting used to a limp.

What helps if your leg still feels different

  • Give it time: wait at least 3 months before deciding whether there is a true difference, because most feelings of a longer leg settle by then.
  • Exercises and physiotherapy: stretching and strengthening help the muscles and pelvis to adjust.
  • A shoe raise: if a true difference remains and you notice it, a small raise inside or under the shoe on the shorter side usually solves it. Most people do not notice a difference of less than about 1 cm.
  • Further surgery: this is rarely needed, and only for a large difference that causes real problems.

When to get in touch

Please contact us if your limp is getting worse rather than better, if you have new pain in the hip or groin, or if you feel the hip is unstable. Persistent pain on the outer side of the hip can have other causes. See pain on the outside of the hip.
