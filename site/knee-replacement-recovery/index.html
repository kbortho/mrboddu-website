Knee replacement recovery: week by week

Recovery from a knee replacement takes time, and the hard work you put into your exercises makes a real difference. This guide shows what most people can expect. Everyone recovers at their own pace, and I will tailor the advice to you.

In hospital: the first 1 to 2 days

  • The physiotherapists will help you stand and walk with a frame or crutches, usually on the day of surgery or the next morning.
  • They will teach you exercises to bend and straighten the knee, and how to manage stairs.
  • Most people go home after 1 to 2 nights, once they are comfortable and walking safely.

Weeks 1 to 2

  • Do your exercises several times a day. Getting the knee fully straight, and bending it a little more each day, is the most important part of recovery.
  • Take your painkillers regularly, especially before exercises, so that you can do them properly.
  • Swelling and bruising are normal. Rest with your leg raised and use a cold pack wrapped in a towel for 15 to 20 minutes at a time.
  • Walk short distances around the house, and a little further each day.
  • At about 2 weeks, your wound will be checked and your clips removed, usually by a nurse at the hospital.

Weeks 2 to 6

  • Most people walk further and more confidently, and may move from crutches to one stick.
  • Night-time aching and poor sleep are common in these weeks. They usually improve.
  • Keep working on bending the knee. Your physiotherapist will check your progress.
  • At about 6 weeks, I will see you for a follow-up appointment.

Weeks 6 to 12

  • Many people walk without crutches or sticks.
  • Most people can drive after about 6 weeks, once they can safely make an emergency stop. Tell your insurer that you have had surgery.
  • Office work is often possible from about 6 weeks. Heavier or physical work may take around 3 months.
  • Gentle swimming, an exercise bike and walking are good ways to build strength.

3 to 12 months

  • Most people are back to most daily activities and many hobbies by about 3 months, such as golf, cycling, bowls and dancing.
  • Some swelling, warmth and stiffness can last for several months. This is normal.
  • The knee keeps improving for up to a year. Kneeling will not damage your new knee, but many people find it uncomfortable at first.

Tips for a good recovery

  • Exercises: little and often is better than a long session once a day. See the knee exercises, with diagrams.
  • Stairs: go up with your good leg first and down with your operated leg first.
  • Pain: some discomfort during exercises is expected, but tell us if pain is getting worse rather than better.
  • Before surgery: getting fitter, strengthening your thigh muscles and preparing your home all help. See your operation.

Get medical advice straight away if you notice:

  • A wound that becomes red, hot or swollen, or leaks fluid
  • A fever or feeling unwell
  • Pain or swelling in your calf
  • Chest pain or breathlessness

In an emergency, call 999.
