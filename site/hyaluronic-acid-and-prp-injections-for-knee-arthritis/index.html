Hyaluronic acid and PRP injections for knee arthritis

Many clinics offer “gel” (hyaluronic acid) or PRP (platelet-rich plasma) injections for knee arthritis, often at a high price. The best and most recent research shows that, for most people, they work no better than a dummy (placebo) injection. Here is what the evidence shows.

The short answer

  • Hyaluronic acid (gel, viscosupplement) injections: large, well-run trials show no worthwhile benefit over placebo, and a small increase in side effects. NICE says they should not be offered.
  • PRP injections: the best-quality trial found no benefit over a salt-water placebo for pain or for the cartilage. NICE says they should only be used within special arrangements or research.
  • Steroid injections: can give short-term relief, usually for a few weeks to a few months. See steroid injections.

Why the evidence has changed

Early, small studies often suggested these injections worked. But small trials, and trials with weaknesses in their design, tend to overestimate benefit. When researchers look only at large, well-designed trials, most of the benefit disappears. Part of the reason is that any injection into the knee, even salt water, can make people feel better for a while: the placebo effect is strong in knee arthritis.

Hyaluronic acid (gel) injections

  • A review in the BMJ in 2022 included 169 trials and more than 21,000 patients. In the large placebo-controlled trials, the reduction in pain was tiny, well below the level that patients would notice. It also found a higher risk of serious side effects than with placebo.
  • A 2025 review of large trials only (57 trials, almost 23,000 patients) found that hyaluronic acid had no effect on pain compared with placebo, and again more side effects.
  • NICE (2022) advises: do not offer hyaluronan injections for osteoarthritis.

PRP (platelet-rich plasma) injections

PRP is made by taking a sample of your blood and spinning it to concentrate the platelets, which are then injected into the knee.

  • The RESTORE trial, published in JAMA in 2021, gave 288 people with knee arthritis either PRP or salt-water injections. After 12 months, there was no difference in pain, function or cartilage on MRI scans.
  • The 2025 review of large trials found that most injected treatments, other than steroid, had effects that were small and consistent with placebo.
  • NICE advises that the evidence on how well PRP works is limited, so it should only be used with special arrangements for consent and audit, or in research.

Other injections

Other injections, such as stem cell or “regenerative” treatments, are sometimes advertised for knee arthritis. They have not been shown in large, good-quality trials to rebuild cartilage or to work better than placebo, and they can be expensive.

What does help

  • Exercise and strengthening: NICE recommends exercise as a core treatment for everyone with knee arthritis. See knee exercises.
  • Weight loss, if you are overweight, reduces the load on the knee.
  • Painkillers, including anti-inflammatory gels applied to the knee.
  • A steroid injection, for short-term relief, for example to help you get going with exercise.
  • Surgery, such as a partial or total knee replacement, when arthritis is severe and other treatments no longer help.

I generally do not recommend hyaluronic acid or PRP injections, because the best evidence shows they are unlikely to help and you may be paying for a placebo. At your consultation, I will talk you through the options that are most likely to work for you.
