Which approach is best for a hip replacement?

The “approach” is the route the surgeon takes through the muscles to reach the hip joint. You may have read that one approach is better than the others. The research does not show this. By about three months after surgery, people do equally well whichever approach is used. Each approach has its own small risks, and the surgeon’s experience with an approach matters more than the approach itself.

The short answer

  • No approach is superior. Trials comparing them find that, by about three months after surgery, function, pain and walking are the same.
  • Each approach carries a slightly different pattern of small risks.
  • I use a small-incision anterolateral approach, which reaches the hip from the front and side.

The three main approaches

  • Posterior approach: the hip is reached from the back. It is the most widely used approach worldwide.
  • Anterior approach (often called the direct anterior approach): the hip is reached from the front, working between the muscles.
  • Anterolateral approach: the hip is reached from the front and side, through a small part of the muscles on the outer side of the hip.

All three can be done through a smaller incision, and all three can be combined with an accelerated recovery programme, so most people are up and walking on the day of surgery or the next morning.

What the research shows about recovery

The anterior approach is often promoted as giving a faster recovery. Some trials do show slightly better scores in the first few weeks, but the difference does not last.

  • A review of 9 randomised trials (754 hip replacements) comparing the anterior and posterior approaches found slightly better hip scores with the anterior approach at 2 and 6 weeks, but no difference at three months or at one year.
  • A 2025 review of 17 randomised trials (1,575 patients) compared the anterior approach with the posterior and lateral approaches. Hip function was slightly better with the anterior approach at one month, but there was no difference at three months or at one year.
  • A 2025 “umbrella” review, which pooled the findings of 11 earlier reviews of randomised trials, reached the same conclusion: any early advantage of the anterior approach had gone by three months.

In other words, whichever approach is used, most people are in the same place by about three months.

The small risks of each approach

Every approach is safe in experienced hands, and serious problems are uncommon with all of them. But each has its own trade-off.

Posterior approach: slightly more dislocations

A dislocation is when the ball of the new hip comes out of the socket. Large registry studies show the posterior approach has a slightly higher risk of dislocation, both early on and in later years. In the Dutch national registry of more than 166,000 hip replacements, the other approaches had about half the risk of needing further surgery for dislocation. In the Norwegian registry, the posterior approach had about twice the risk compared with a lateral approach. Larger ball sizes and careful repair of the soft tissues reduce this risk.

Anterior approach: thigh numbness and early fractures

  • Numbness on the front of the thigh: a skin nerve (the lateral cutaneous nerve of the thigh) runs close to the incision. In one study, about 8 in 10 people reported some numbness or tingling on the front of the thigh a year later. It is usually mild and does not affect how the hip works, but it can be permanent.
  • Fracture around the implant: the anterior approach gives a narrower view of the thigh bone, and studies have found a slightly higher risk of an early fracture or loosening around the stem. In a study of early failures from three US centres, half of the early stem failures were after the anterior approach.

The latest report of the Australian national joint registry, which has compared more than 360,000 hip replacements since 2015, confirms this pattern. The anterior approach has a higher rate of revision for loosening and for early fracture, but a lower rate of revision for infection and dislocation.

Anterolateral approach: a small risk of abductor weakness

The abductor muscles on the outer side of the hip hold the pelvis level when you walk. Because approaches from the side work close to these muscles, there is a small risk that they are weaker afterwards, which can cause a limp. Careful handling and repair of the muscles keeps this risk low, and in most people any weakness improves with exercises over the first few months.

The approach I use

I use a small-incision anterolateral approach. It gives me a good view of both the socket and the thigh bone, which helps me position the implants accurately, and it has a low risk of dislocation. In the last 10 years, none of my patients has had a dislocation after a first-time (primary) hip replacement. This does not mean a dislocation can never happen, but it reflects a careful technique.

My overall results are also good. The National Joint Registry records every hip replacement I perform. Its data for the last ten years show that my patients have needed fewer than a third of the revisions (repeat operations) expected, after allowing for their age and health. See my outcomes and patient reviews.

I do not routinely ask patients to follow the traditional “hip precautions” (restrictions on bending and sitting in low chairs). See recovery after a hip replacement.

What matters more than the approach

The things that make the biggest difference to your result are the surgeon’s experience, accurate positioning of the implants, the choice of implant, and your own preparation and rehabilitation. When you choose a surgeon, it is reasonable to ask which approach they use, how often they use it, and what their own results are.

Talking it through

At your consultation I will explain how I would perform your operation, the risks that apply to you and what to expect during recovery. You are always welcome to ask about other approaches.
