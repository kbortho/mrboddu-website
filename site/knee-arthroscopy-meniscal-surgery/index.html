Knee arthroscopy and meniscal surgery

Knee arthroscopy is keyhole surgery using a small camera. I use it to treat meniscal tears that cause locking or persistent symptoms, and I repair the meniscus where possible.

At a glance

Hospital stay
Usually a day case
Anaesthetic
Usually a general anaesthetic
After trimming
Desk work after about 1 to 2 weeks
After repair
A brace and protected movement for about 6 weeks

When can keyhole surgery help?

  • A torn piece of meniscus that makes the knee lock or catch
  • A tear from an injury that can be repaired, especially in younger and active people
  • Symptoms from a tear that continue despite a good course of physiotherapy

When it is unlikely to help

Keyhole surgery does not treat knee arthritis, and national guidance advises against it for arthritis alone. For tears that come on gradually in middle age, physiotherapy is usually the first treatment, because good-quality trials show that surgery often adds little. I will tell you if I think surgery is unlikely to help you.

Repair or trimming?

The meniscus protects the knee, so I repair it where the tear is suitable. Repairs heal best in younger patients and in tears near the outer edge, which have a good blood supply. Where a tear cannot be repaired, I trim away the torn part (meniscal debridement) and keep as much healthy meniscus as possible.

Recovery

After trimming, most people walk the same day and return to desk work within 1 to 2 weeks. After a repair, recovery is longer, usually with a brace and limited bending for about 6 weeks, and a return to sport after 4 to 6 months.

Risks

Risks are low but include infection, blood clots, swelling, stiffness and nerve injury. Some meniscal repairs do not heal and need further surgery.
