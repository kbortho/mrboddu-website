Blood clots after a hip or knee replacement

Blood clots in the leg (deep vein thrombosis) or lungs (pulmonary embolism) are an important risk after any major leg operation. With modern prevention they are uncommon. Here is how the risk is reduced, and the signs to look out for.

How clots are prevented

Your prevention plan follows national guidance (NICE) and is tailored to you after your pre-operative assessment.

  • Getting moving early: walking on the day of surgery or the next day is one of the best ways to prevent clots.
  • Blood-thinning medicine: based on the best available evidence, most people have 2 weeks of injections after a knee replacement. After a hip replacement, most people have 10 days of injections followed by 28 days of tablets. Individual factors are also taken into account, so your own plan may differ.
  • Drinking plenty and doing your ankle and leg exercises.

A large Australian trial (CRISTAL) of more than 9,000 patients found fewer symptomatic blood clots with injections than with aspirin alone after hip and knee replacement. Please complete the full course of blood-thinning medicine, even when you feel well.

Who is at higher risk?

The risk is higher if you have had a clot before, have a family history of clots, take hormone medicines such as some contraceptive pills or HRT, have cancer, or are less mobile. Tell us at your pre-operative assessment if any of these apply.

Get medical advice straight away if you notice pain, swelling, warmth or tenderness in your calf or thigh.

Call 999 if you have sudden breathlessness, chest pain, or cough up blood.

Travel

Long journeys increase the risk of clots. For long-haul flights, it is usually best to wait about 3 months after surgery. See life after a joint replacement.
