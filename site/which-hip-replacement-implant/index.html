Which hip replacement implant is best?

A hip replacement has four main parts, and there are many brands and materials to choose from. There is no single “best” implant for everyone. What matters most is choosing well-proven parts with a long track record, and fixing them in the way that suits your bone. Here is how I make that choice.

The short answer

  • I use implants with a long, proven track record in the National Joint Registry.
  • The bearing (the moving surfaces) is usually a ceramic ball on a cross-linked polyethylene liner.
  • The socket is an uncemented titanium-alloy cup. The stem is uncemented if your bone is strong, and cemented if it is not.

The parts of a hip replacement

  • The cup: a metal shell (titanium alloy) fixed into the socket of the pelvis.
  • The liner: fits inside the cup and forms the new socket surface.
  • The stem: fixed into the thigh bone.
  • The ball (head): fits on top of the stem and moves inside the liner.

The bearing: ceramic on cross-linked polyethylene

The ball and liner rub against each other with every step, so the materials matter. The main choices are a ceramic or metal ball, and a polyethylene (a very hard-wearing plastic) or ceramic liner.

  • Cross-linked polyethylene is a toughened polyethylene that wears much more slowly than older types. Long-term registry studies show that it greatly reduces the need for a revision (repeat operation) caused by wear.
  • Ceramic on cross-linked polyethylene is one of the most successful bearings. The UK National Joint Registry, which now includes about 1.8 million hip replacements, reports that ceramic-on-polyethylene hips have had consistently low revision rates for up to 21 years, however the implants are fixed.
  • I use a ceramic ball on a cross-linked polyethylene liner for most patients. Sometimes I choose a metal ball, for example in older patients, or when it suits the implant sizes needed for your hip. Both are well-proven combinations.

Metal-on-metal hips, which caused problems in the past, are no longer used, and I do not use them.

Fixation: cemented or uncemented?

Implants are fixed to the bone in one of two ways:

  • Uncemented: the implant has a rough, coated titanium-alloy surface, and it is pressed firmly into the bone. Over the following weeks, the bone grows onto the surface and holds it in place.
  • Cemented: the implant is fixed with bone cement, a type of acrylic that sets hard within minutes.

Both methods work very well. The right choice depends mainly on the quality of your bone.

  • The cup: I use an uncemented titanium-alloy cup.
  • The stem: if your bone is strong, I use an uncemented titanium-alloy stem. If your bone is softer or thinner, which is more common with age and in women, I use a cemented stem.

The evidence supports tailoring the choice in this way. Uncemented stems need strong bone to grip. In older patients, the large Australian and German national registries show a higher revision rate with uncemented stems, mainly because of fractures around the implant. In the German registry, cemented stems had the lowest revision rate in patients over 80.

Why I choose implants with a long track record

A new implant design can look good in its first few years and still perform less well later. My own research, using National Joint Registry data, found that results in the first 3 to 5 years are a poor guide to how a hip replacement will perform at 15 years. For this reason, I use implants with a long, proven record in the National Joint Registry. Registry figures show that 95 in 100 hip replacements using the implants I use last more than 15 years. See how long hip and knee replacements last.

What about robotic surgery and the approach?

How the implants are put in matters as much as which implants are used. Accurate positioning of the cup and stem, and the right leg length and stability, are important for a good result. I use Mako robotic assistance only where it is appropriate. See robotic hip and knee replacement and which approach is best for a hip replacement.

Talking it through

At your consultation, I will look at your X-rays and explain which implants I recommend for you, and why. You are always welcome to ask about the implants and their track record.
