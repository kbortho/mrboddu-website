Driving after a hip or knee replacement

Most people can drive again about 6 weeks after a hip or knee replacement. The real test is not the date, but whether you can control the car safely, including making an emergency stop.

The short answer

  • Most people can drive after about 6 weeks.
  • You must be able to make an emergency stop without hesitating, and get in and out of the car comfortably.
  • Do not drive if strong painkillers make you drowsy.
  • Tell your insurance company that you have had an operation.

What the research shows

Studies have measured how quickly people can press the brake pedal after surgery. A review of 19 studies found that braking time was back to normal about 2 weeks after a right hip replacement and about 4 weeks after a right knee replacement.

Braking time is only part of driving safely. You also need enough strength and comfort to sit for a while, turn to look over your shoulder, and get in and out of the car. That is why I advise most people to wait about 6 weeks, and some people need longer.

Does it matter which leg?

  • Right leg: this is the leg you brake with, so be sure you can press the brake hard and fast before you drive.
  • Left leg, manual car: you need to be able to press the clutch fully and quickly.
  • Left leg, automatic car: you may be able to drive a little sooner, because the left leg does little work. You still need to be comfortable and safe, and able to get in and out.

How to know you are ready

  • You are walking well, without crutches or with only one stick.
  • You can get in and out of the car without help.
  • You can sit comfortably for the length of the journey.
  • You are not taking painkillers that make you drowsy or slow.
  • You can press the pedals firmly, including an emergency stop. Try this first in a stationary car.

Start with a short journey on quiet roads, ideally with another driver in the car.

Insurance and the law

It is your responsibility to be in control of the car. Tell your insurer that you have had surgery, as policies differ. Strong painkillers such as codeine or morphine can affect driving: do not drive if you feel drowsy, dizzy or slow to react.

Being a passenger

You can be a passenger as soon as you leave hospital. Push the seat back and recline it a little to make getting in easier, and stop every hour on long journeys to walk around.
